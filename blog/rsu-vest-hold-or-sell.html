RSUs at vest: hold or sell?

Every vest forces a decision, even if you never make one. Leaving the shares where they land is a choice to invest that money in your employer. A simple test helps: if the company had paid you cash instead, would you buy this much of its stock?

Alex J. Potenza, CFP®, CPWA®
Vice President & Director of Financial Planning

Why selling at vest usually costs little in tax

When RSUs vest, their full market value is taxed as ordinary income, the same as a cash bonus. That income is taxed whether you sell the shares or not. Your cost basis in the shares becomes their value on the vest date.

So if you sell soon after vest, there is usually little or no additional tax: only the gain or loss between the vest date and the sale. The tax that matters has already been triggered. Our post on RSU tax withholding covers why the amount withheld at vest often falls short of that bill.

The long-term capital gains myth

A common reason for holding is to "get long-term capital gains." Holding for more than a year after vest does qualify any further growth for long-term rates. It does nothing for the value at vest, which is already taxed as ordinary income. The tax benefit of holding applies only to future appreciation, and you take on the full risk of the shares to get it.

The bonus test

Picture the vest as a cash bonus deposited into your account. Would you use all of it to buy your employer's stock today? If the answer is no, holding the RSUs is the same decision you just declined, made by default.

The test is useful because it removes two biases: the feeling that the shares are "free" and the habit of keeping things where they arrive.

Concentration and your paycheck

Your job and your company stock depend on the same business. If the company struggles, your shares can fall at the same time your bonus shrinks, your unvested grants lose value, or your role is at risk. That correlation is the core reason diversification matters more for employee shareholders than for outside investors.

Many planners set a ceiling on employer stock as a share of net worth, often expressed as a percentage, and treat anything above it as a candidate for sale. The right ceiling depends on your other assets, your income stability, and your goals.

Reasons people reasonably hold

  • Ownership guidelines. Many companies require executives to hold a set value of stock. Selling below that level may not be allowed.
  • Planned charitable gifts. Shares held more than a year after vest that have appreciated can be donated, often with a deduction for their market value and no capital gains tax on the appreciation.
  • A deliberate, sized position. Some people choose to keep a defined amount of company stock as part of a diversified portfolio, with a written limit.

Each of these is a decision with a reason and a limit. Holding everything because selling feels disloyal or because the stock "has to come back" is not.

How to sell well

Choose your lots

If you hold shares from several vests, each lot has its own basis and holding period. Selling with specific identification lets you choose high-basis lots first to minimize gain, or harvest a loss. Set this up with your broker before the trade; a default method may choose for you.

Mind the trading windows

Insiders can usually trade only during open windows under the company's insider trading policy. Affiliates face Rule 144 conditions as well. A 10b5-1 plan can schedule sales at vest so they happen without a decision each quarter.

Decide where the proceeds go

Selling only helps if the money is put to work: an emergency reserve, a tax payment, a down payment, or a diversified portfolio. Decide before the vest.

Hypothetical example: Meet "Jordan," a director at a public company with RSUs vesting every quarter. Jordan sets a rule: sell each vest at the next open window, except for enough shares to meet the company's ownership guideline. The rule turns twelve decisions a year into one.

Common mistakes

  • Holding to "get long-term capital gains" on income that is already taxed.
  • Letting vests accumulate until company stock dominates your net worth.
  • Selling without choosing tax lots.
  • Forgetting that withholding at vest may not cover the full tax.
  • Reporting the 1099-B cost basis without adjusting it. See what to give your CPA.

Frequently asked

Frequently asked

Should I sell my RSUs as soon as they vest?+

It depends on your goals, but the tax cost of selling at vest is usually small, because the vest value is already taxed as ordinary income. Holding is effectively a decision to invest that amount in your employer, so weigh it the way you would buying the stock with cash.

Are RSUs taxed twice?+

No, but they can appear to be. The value at vest is ordinary income, and only the change in value after vest is capital gain or loss when you sell. If the 1099-B cost basis is reported incorrectly and not adjusted, the vest income can be taxed a second time.

Do I get long-term capital gains if I hold RSUs for a year?+

Only on appreciation after the vest date. The value at vest is taxed as ordinary income regardless of how long you hold. Holding more than a year qualifies further gains for long-term rates, while exposing the full position to the stock's risk.

How much company stock is too much?+

There is no single answer. Many planners set a limit on employer stock as a percentage of net worth, considering that your income and unvested awards already depend on the same company. The appropriate limit depends on your other assets, income stability, and goals.

Can executives sell RSUs whenever they want?+

Usually not. Insiders typically trade only in open windows under the company's insider trading policy, and affiliates must also meet Rule 144 conditions. A 10b5-1 plan can schedule sales in advance so they occur even outside open windows, where company policy allows.
