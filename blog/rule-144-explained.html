Rule 144: selling restricted and control stock.

If you are an officer or director, or you hold shares from a private placement or acquisition, you can't simply sell whenever you like. Rule 144 sets the conditions. Understanding them early is what turns a stock sale into a schedule rather than a surprise.

Alex J. Potenza, CFP®, CPWA®
Vice President & Director of Financial Planning

What is Rule 144?

Under the Securities Act of 1933, every sale of securities has to be registered with the SEC or fit an exemption. Most open-market trades rely on an exemption without anyone thinking about it. Rule 144 is the exemption, technically a safe harbor, that lets two specific groups resell shares publicly without registration: holders of restricted securities and holders of control securities.

If you meet its conditions, you are not treated as an "underwriter" distributing stock to the public, and the sale can go through. If you do not, the sale may need to be registered or fit some other exemption.

Who it applies to

Restricted securities

Restricted securities are shares acquired in an unregistered transaction. Common sources include private placements, pre-IPO stock purchases, shares received as consideration when a private company is acquired, and some founder and early-employee grants. These certificates or book entries usually carry a restrictive legend.

Control securities

Control securities are any shares held by an affiliate of the company: a person who controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with the issuer. Directors, executive officers, and large shareholders are commonly treated as affiliates, although the determination depends on the facts and is usually made by company counsel.

This is the part executives most often miss. Shares from RSUs and options granted under a company's registered equity plan (typically registered on Form S-8) are not restricted securities. But if you are an affiliate, those same shares are control securities, and your sales still have to follow Rule 144's other conditions.

The holding period

For restricted securities, the holding period runs from the date the shares were bought and fully paid for:

  • Six months if the issuer has been an SEC reporting company for at least 90 days.
  • One year if the issuer is not a reporting company.

Control securities bought in the open market or received under a registered plan have no holding period. The affiliate conditions below still apply.

After the holding period, a non-affiliate holding restricted securities of a reporting company can generally sell, subject only to the current public information requirement until a full year has passed. After one year, a non-affiliate can generally sell without further Rule 144 conditions. Someone who was an affiliate is treated as a non-affiliate once they have not been one for 90 days.

The conditions for affiliates

Affiliates selling either restricted or control securities must meet four further conditions on every sale.

Current public information

The company must be current in its SEC reporting. If it falls behind on its filings, affiliate sales under Rule 144 generally stop until it catches up.

Volume limit

In any three-month period, an affiliate may sell no more than the greater of:

  • 1% of the shares of that class outstanding, or
  • the average weekly reported trading volume during the four calendar weeks before the Form 144 is filed (for listed securities).

The limit counts sales by certain related parties together. That can include family members sharing your home and some trusts and entities you control, so the share count is a household question rather than an individual one.

Hypothetical example: Meet "David," a director at a public company with 200 million shares outstanding. One percent is 2,000,000 shares. If average weekly volume over the prior four weeks was 1,500,000 shares, the higher figure, 2,000,000 shares, sets his maximum for any three-month period. For most executives the limit never binds. For founders and large holders, it can set the pace of diversification more than taxes do.

Manner of sale

Equity securities must be sold in ordinary brokers' transactions, directly with a market maker, or in riskless principal transactions. The seller and broker may not solicit buyers or pay more than the usual commission.

Form 144

An affiliate must file Form 144 with the SEC if sales in any three-month period exceed 5,000 shares or $50,000 in aggregate sale price. Since April 2023 the form has been filed electronically on EDGAR, and it is due no later than when the sell order is placed. In practice your broker's restricted stock desk usually prepares it with you.

How Rule 144 fits with a 10b5-1 plan

The two rules solve different problems. A 10b5-1 plan provides a defense against insider trading claims. Rule 144 governs whether the resale itself needs registration. An affiliate selling under a 10b5-1 plan still has to satisfy Rule 144, including the volume limit and Form 144. The form includes a place to disclose the plan's adoption date.

Section 16 reporting on Form 4 and the short-swing profit rule are separate again. One sale by an executive can touch all three.

Removing the restrictive legend

Meeting the conditions is not the same as being able to sell. If your shares carry a restrictive legend, the transfer agent usually needs an opinion from counsel, often the company's, before the legend comes off. That takes time, and the request typically goes through the company's legal team. Build the lead time into any plan that depends on a sale date.

Turning the rules into a selling plan

Rule 144 tells you what you are allowed to sell. The financial plan decides what you should sell and when. A few questions shape that schedule:

  • How long will diversification take? If the volume limit binds, the answer is a schedule measured in quarters or years, and the plan should say so.
  • Which lots go first? Shares with the smallest embedded gains generally cost the least tax to sell. Low-basis shares may be better used for charitable giving.
  • How do the sales land across tax years? Spreading sales across calendar years gives more room to manage brackets and the net investment income tax. See our page on tax-aware strategies.
  • Where do the proceeds go? Decide the reinvestment plan before the first trade, not after.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming shares from a registered equity plan are freely tradeable when you are an affiliate.
  • Counting only your own sales toward the volume limit and forgetting household members and controlled entities.
  • Forgetting the Form 144 filing, or leaving it until the day of the trade.
  • Planning a sale date without allowing time for legend removal and company approval.
  • Treating Rule 144 compliance as the whole plan instead of one constraint inside it.

Frequently asked

Frequently asked

What is Rule 144 in simple terms?+

An SEC safe harbor that lets holders of restricted securities and company affiliates resell shares publicly without registering the sale, provided they meet conditions such as a holding period, current public information, volume limits, manner-of-sale rules, and a Form 144 filing.

What is the Rule 144 holding period?+

For restricted securities, six months if the issuer has been an SEC reporting company for at least 90 days, and one year if it has not. Control securities bought in the open market or received under a registered plan have no holding period, though affiliates still face the other conditions.

Does Rule 144 apply to my RSUs?+

Shares from RSUs granted under a registered equity plan are not restricted securities. If you are an affiliate, such as a director or executive officer, they are control securities, so your sales must still meet Rule 144's volume, manner-of-sale, public information, and Form 144 requirements.

What is the Rule 144 volume limit?+

In any three-month period, an affiliate may sell no more than the greater of 1% of the class outstanding or the average weekly reported trading volume over the four calendar weeks before filing Form 144. Sales by certain family members and controlled entities are counted together.

When do I need to file Form 144?+

An affiliate files Form 144 electronically on EDGAR when sales in a three-month period exceed 5,000 shares or $50,000 in aggregate sale price. The form is due no later than when the sell order is placed, and brokers commonly help prepare it.

Does a 10b5-1 plan replace Rule 144?+

No. A 10b5-1 plan is a defense against insider trading claims; Rule 144 governs whether a resale needs registration. Affiliates selling under a 10b5-1 plan still need to meet Rule 144's conditions, including the volume limit and Form 144 filing.
